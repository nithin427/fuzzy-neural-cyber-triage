\title{Fuzzy--Neural Decision Intelligence for Cost-Aware Cyber Alert Triage\tnoteref{t1}}
\tnotetext[t1]{Article type: Short Communication.}

\author[mu]{Sai Nithin Erigineni\corref{cor1}}
\ead{serigineni@midwest.edu}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[mu]{DBA, Midwest University, 851 Parr Rd, Wentzville, MO 63385, United States}

\begin{abstract}
Security operations centres (SOCs) receive risk scores from machine-learning detectors, but the step from score to action usually rests on a single hand-set threshold. This paper treats alert triage as a knowledge-based decision problem. A multilayer perceptron (MLP) estimates attack propensity, fuzzy membership functions map it to Low, Medium and High risk bands, and three production rules assign Monitor, Soft-block or Escalate. On 20,000 NSL-KDD test alerts, gradient boosting is the stronger detector (ROC-AUC 0.947 against 0.908). On the same MLP scores, however, the rule base lowers the mean triage cost to 3.068 (95\% CI [3.016, 3.118]), compared with 3.264 for a 0.5 threshold and 4.309 for top-20\% escalation (both Wilcoxon $p<10^{-100}$). It also raises attack coverage from 0.655 to 0.686. The rule base is cheaper than both crisp rules in all ten repeated runs ($p=0.002$) and cheaper than the threshold at every tested missed-attack cost from 2 to 30. Ablations show that the gain comes from the Soft-block band and that a Bayes cost rule is cheaper still (2.164). The contribution is an auditable decision layer, not a new detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Security operations centres (SOCs) collect, analyse and respond to an organisation's security alerts~\cite{vielberth2020}. Their analysts receive far more alerts than they can investigate, and the resulting alert fatigue is a central operational risk~\cite{tariq2025,chhetri2024}. Intrusion-detection research has progressed from classical data mining~\cite{liao2013,buczak2016} to deep networks trained on benchmarks such as NSL-KDD~\cite{vinayakumar2019,su2020}. Most of this work, however, ends with a risk score and judges it by accuracy or by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). A SOC manager faces a different question: with limited analyst capacity and unequal error costs, which alerts should be monitored, contained or escalated? This is a data-driven decision task rather than a prediction task~\cite{phillipswren2021,duan2019}. Decision-theoretic three-way decisions already choose among acceptance, rejection and deferment under stated losses~\cite{yao2010}, and recent SOC studies learn when to defer alerts to analysts~\cite{jalalvand2027} or prioritise them by reinforcement learning~\cite{chavali2024}, while research on SOC human--AI teaming~\cite{kumar2026} and concept drift~\cite{vaarandi2026} addresses the operating context.

Fuzzy and neuro-fuzzy systems suit this decision step, because they express learned evidence as linguistic rules that operators can read and audit~\cite{zadeh1965,jang1993,souza2020,fernandez2019}. As Table~\ref{tab:rw} shows, existing studies use these strengths for other purposes. Fuzzy intrusion detectors use rules to improve accuracy, but they still output a binary alarm~\cite{toosi2007,hamamoto2018,alalhareth2023}. Cost-sensitive learning~\cite{elkan2001,zhou2006} enters intrusion detection mainly through weighted training losses~\cite{gupta2022,telikani2024}, so the cost model shapes the classifier but does not choose the action. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methods explain a score after it has been produced~\cite{arrieta2020,wang2020xai,alabbadi2025}. Reinforcement learning can rank alerts by priority, yet the ranking does not assign an action~\cite{chavali2024,wang2024alertpro}. The closest designs to ours defer uncertain cases. Three-way decision models assign each case to acceptance, rejection or deferment, with thresholds derived from loss functions~\cite{yao2010}, and Jalalvand et al.~\cite{jalalvand2027} learn when to defer alerts from analyst feedback. Neither assigns graded SOC actions through a readable fuzzy rule base driven by a learned score. Knowledge-based decision support has shown the value of explicit domain knowledge in cyber-risk assessment~\cite{bag2025}. However, to our knowledge none of these studies combines an action-cost model, readable rules and graded responses in one triage layer.

This Short Communication addresses that gap with three contributions. It proposes a knowledge-based layer that maps neural attack propensity to fuzzy risk bands and then to SOC actions through explicit rules (Section~\ref{sec:method}). It evaluates the layer by the quality of its decisions, using paired tests, repeated runs and sensitivity analyses (Sections~\ref{sec:design} and~\ref{sec:results}). Finally, ablations show where the gain comes from and where it ends.

\section{Proposed fuzzy--neural decision framework}
\label{sec:method}
\textit{Neural propensity model.} Each alert $i$ has a feature vector $x_i$ and a label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, where 1 denotes an attack. The 38 numeric NSL-KDD fields are standardised, and the protocol, service and flag fields are one-hot encoded. An MLP $g_\theta$ with two ReLU layers of width 128 and 64, a dropout rate of 0.2 and a single logit output estimates the attack propensity:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
p_i&=\sigma\big(g_\theta(x_i)\big),\\
\mathcal{L}(\theta)&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[w_{+}\,y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big],\qquad w_{+}=\frac{N_{-}}{N_{+}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:mlp}
\end{equation}
where $N_{-}$ and $N_{+}$ are the numbers of normal and attack training records, so that $w_{+}$ offsets the class imbalance. The network is trained with Adam at a learning rate of $10^{-3}$ and a batch size of 512 for 20 epochs, and the epoch with the best validation ROC-AUC is kept.

\textit{Fuzzy risk bands and production rules.} Two shoulder functions and one triangular function map the propensity to three linguistic risk bands~\cite{zadeh1965}. With the clipping operator $[z]_0^1=\min(1,\max(0,z))$, the membership functions are
\begin{align}
\mu_L(p)&=\big[(0.45-p)/0.45\big]_0^1, \label{eq:muL}\\
\mu_M(p)&=\begin{cases}\big[(p-0.15)/0.35\big]_0^1, & p<0.5,\\ \big[(0.85-p)/0.35\big]_0^1, & p\ge 0.5,\end{cases} \label{eq:muM}\\
\mu_H(p)&=\big[(p-0.55)/0.45\big]_0^1. \label{eq:muH}
\end{align}
The knowledge layer is the production-rule base $\mathcal{R}=\{R_1,R_2,R_3\}$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.88\linewidth}@{}}
$R_1$: & IF risk IS Low THEN Monitor: log the alert without intervention.\\
$R_2$: & IF risk IS Medium THEN Soft-block: apply automatic containment.\\
$R_3$: & IF risk IS High THEN Escalate: hand the alert to a human analyst.
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Each rule $R_k$ fires with strength $\mu_k(p_i)$. The strongest rule determines the action, and a tie goes to the less severe action:
\begin{equation}
a_i = A\big(k_i^{*}\big),\qquad k_i^{*}=\arg\max_{k\in\{L,M,H\}}\mu_k(p_i),
\label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}
where $A$ maps $L$, $M$ and $H$ to Monitor, Soft-block and Escalate. The firing strengths are logged with every decision and serve as its explanation. For example, an alert with $p=0.60$ has $\mu_M=0.71$ and $\mu_H=0.11$, so it is soft-blocked. Solving $\mu_L=\mu_M$ and $\mu_M=\mu_H$ shows that the rule base switches action at $p=9/32=0.281$ and $p=23/32=0.719$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bands}). The breakpoints were set by expert judgement before the test data were examined.

The membership functions of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:muL})--(\ref{eq:muH}) and rules $R_1$--$R_3$ switch action at $p=9/32$ and $p=23/32$ (dotted lines). Dashed: Bayes soft-block threshold $p=2/11$. \textit{Cost model and decision metrics.} Each action is scored with the relative cost matrix
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C(\text{Monitor},y)&=C_{FN}\,y, & C(\text{Soft-block},y)&=1+(1-y),\\
C(\text{Escalate},y)&=3+3(1-y), & C_{FN}&=10.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
Each policy is evaluated on three metrics. The mean triage cost is $\bar C=\frac1N\sum_i C(a_i,y_i)$. The attack coverage, $\sum_i \mathbf{1}[a_i\neq\text{Monitor}]\,y_i/\sum_i y_i$, is the share of attacks that receive any action other than Monitor. The third metric counts unnecessary escalations (UE), which are benign alerts sent to an analyst. As a reference, we also report the plug-in Bayes cost rule
\begin{equation}
a_i^{B}=\arg\min_{a}\;\big[(1-p_i)\,C(a,0)+p_i\,C(a,1)\big].
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
This rule treats $p_i$ as a calibrated probability, although deep-model scores do not guarantee calibration~\cite{abdar2021}. Because soft-blocking costs less than escalation for both classes, the Bayes rule never escalates. Instead, it soft-blocks every alert with $p_i>2/11$.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}
\textit{Data.} The experiments used the NSL-KDD benchmark~\cite{tavallaee2009}. For reproducibility on a standard CPU, we sampled 40,000 training records and split them, with stratification, into 34,000 training and 6,000 validation records. The scaler and encoder were fitted on the training split only. The test set was capped at 20,000 records with an attack rate of 0.568. Because it contains attack types absent from training, the evaluation includes a realistic distribution shift.

\textit{Baselines.} Three detectors were compared: class-balanced logistic regression, gradient boosting (GB)~\cite{friedman2001} with scikit-learn defaults, and the MLP alone. The proposed policy uses the MLP propensity, so any benefit must come from better decisions rather than better scores. Three decision baselines acted on the same scores. The first escalates every alert scored above 0.5 and monitors the rest. The second tunes this threshold on validation data to minimise cost, a form of threshold moving~\cite{zhou2006}. The third escalates the 20\% highest-scored alerts, mimicking a fixed analyst capacity. Learned policies~\cite{chavali2024,wang2024alertpro} were excluded because they need interaction or logged decision data with outcome feedback, which the benchmark lacks.

\textit{Statistics.} On the seed-42 test set, we report percentile 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) from 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of alerts~\cite{efron1994}. Per-alert cost differences were tested with the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945}, which treats alerts as independent. The full pipeline was repeated for seeds 42--51, and the seed-level costs were compared with a paired Wilcoxon test. Two sweeps varied the missed-attack cost $C_{FN}$ from 2 to 30 and the half-width of the Medium band from 0.25 to 0.45.

\textit{Prediction versus decision.} Gradient boosting is the best detector on every metric (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Panel A), and it beats the MLP in ROC-AUC in all ten seeds, so this study makes no claim about detection. On identical MLP scores, however, the rule base lowers the mean triage cost by 0.196 per alert relative to the 0.5 threshold and by 1.242 relative to top-20\% escalation (Panel B1). It also raises coverage by 0.031 relative to the threshold (95\% CI [0.028, 0.034]). The rule base and the threshold differ only on the 776 Medium-band alerts, which the rule base contains instead of monitoring or escalating them. This small band saves about 3,900 cost units per 20,000 alerts and sends 387 fewer alerts to analysts. Top-20\% escalation yields the fewest unnecessary escalations (47), but it covers only 0.348 of attacks.

\textit{Robustness.} These gains are stable across repeated runs and cost settings. Over ten seeds, the rule base has a mean cost of $3.088\pm0.081$, compared with $3.246\pm0.076$ for the threshold and $4.318\pm0.009$ for top-20\% escalation. It is cheaper than both crisp rules in every seed (Wilcoxon $p=0.002$ for both), and its coverage exceeds that of the threshold in every seed. Its advantage over the threshold grows from 0.055 at $C_{FN}=2$ to 0.549 at $C_{FN}=30$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}). Top-20\% escalation is cheaper only when $C_{FN}\le3$, for example 1.347 against 1.640 at $C_{FN}=2$, because acting on fewer alerts pays off when missed attacks are cheap. Widening the Medium half-width from 0.25 to 0.45 enlarges the Soft-block band from 646 to 900 alerts and lowers the cost steadily from 3.101 to 3.034. Every width beats both crisp rules.

\textit{Sources and limits of the gain.} Crisp bands with cut-points at $p=9/32$ and $23/32$ reproduce every action of the rule base (Panel B2), so the benefit comes from the intermediate containment band rather than from fuzziness as such. What fuzziness adds is a graded explanation of each decision. The rule base is also not cost-optimal. The Bayes cost rule is 0.903 cheaper per alert, because soft-blocking dominates escalation under Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}), and it therefore soft-blocks 8,631 alerts, or 43\% of all traffic. This is the Bayesian minimum-risk procedure from which decision-theoretic three-way decisions derive their loss-based thresholds~\cite{yao2010}. Threshold tuning does not match the rule base either. Validation tuning moves the threshold only to $t=0.53$, at a cost of 3.278, presumably because unseen attack types shift the test scores. Only an oracle threshold of 0.10 or lower, chosen on the test set, would win, with a minimum cost of 2.929 at $t=0.01$. This agrees with the cost-sensitive learning result that asymmetric costs move the optimal cut-off away from 0.5~\cite{elkan2001}. Finally, the layer transfers to another detector. On GB scores, the same rules cost 3.004, which is lower than on MLP scores ($p=0.017$), and they cut unnecessary escalations from 603 to 222.

\textit{Implications for SOC decision-makers.} Detectors should be judged by the decisions they enable, since the action rule alone changed the cost by up to 1.242 per alert. Because the cost matrix sets the mix of actions, organisations should price false containment before automating Soft-block. Unlike a deferral band that hands uncertain alerts to analysts~\cite{jalalvand2027}, the Soft-block band acts automatically, so the 387 alerts it keeps from the analyst queue carry an explicit price for false containment (Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost})). Conformal classification with a reject option~\cite{szabadvary2025} instead abstains on uncertain cases under distribution-free error-rate guarantees rather than a cost matrix, and combining the two is a natural extension. The band boundaries give managers a workload lever that needs no retraining, which matters because NIDS alert streams are reported to be dominated by false positives~\cite{albasheer2022}. Named rules with logged firing strengths give each action an audit trail that post hoc attributions lack~\cite{wang2020xai,alabbadi2025}, which supports compliance and human--AI teaming~\cite{chhetri2024,kumar2026}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
The study has four main limitations. First, fixed bands respond to the distribution of the scores, not only to their ranking. Seed 48, the weakest replicate (cost 3.282, coverage 0.626), has a typical ROC-AUC of 0.900 but the lowest accuracy at the 0.5 threshold (0.757). Adaptive tuning in the style of ANFIS~\cite{jang1993}, or bands derived from the cost matrix, could reduce this sensitivity and narrow the gap to the Bayes rule. Second, NSL-KDD is a laboratory benchmark from 2009 that does not reflect modern enterprise, cloud or Internet of Things traffic~\cite{ring2019,algaradi2020}, and the data were subsampled under a single protocol. Third, the cost matrix is illustrative rather than elicited from a real SOC, and the coverage metric counts containment as covering an attack. Fourth, the study involved no deployment, drift analysis~\cite{vaarandi2026} or user study.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
In conclusion, a fuzzy--neural rule base can serve as a knowledge-based decision layer for alert triage. On NSL-KDD, it achieved lower cost and higher coverage than crisp threshold and capacity rules across seeds, cost ratios and band widths, and its gain comes from the intermediate containment band. Future work will derive the bands from costs elicited in real SOCs and validate the layer in live operation. It will also add distribution-free error guarantees for the automatic actions through conformal classification with a reject option~\cite{szabadvary2025}.
