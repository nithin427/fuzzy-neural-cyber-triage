\title{Fuzzy--Neural Decision Intelligence for Cost-Aware Cyber Alert Triage\tnoteref{t1}}
\tnotetext[t1]{Article type: Short Communication.}

\author[mu]{Sai Nithin Erigineni\corref{cor1}}

\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\address[mu]{DBA, Midwest University, 851 Parr Rd, Wentzville, MO 63385, United States}

\begin{abstract}
Security operations centres (SOCs) receive risk scores from machine-learning detectors, yet the step from score to action is usually a single hand-set threshold. We treat alert triage as a knowledge-based decision problem: a multilayer perceptron (MLP) estimates attack propensity, fuzzy membership functions map it to Low, Medium and High risk bands, and three production rules assign Monitor, Soft-block or Escalate under an explicit cost matrix. On NSL-KDD (20,000 test alerts), gradient boosting is the stronger detector (ROC-AUC 0.947 versus 0.908), but on the same MLP scores the rule base lowers the mean triage cost to 3.068 (95\% CI [3.016, 3.118]) from 3.264 for a 0.5 threshold and 4.309 for top-20\% escalation, and raises attack coverage from 0.655 to 0.686 (Wilcoxon $p<10^{-100}$). It is cheaper than both crisp rules in all ten repeated runs ($p=0.002$) and than the threshold at every tested missed-attack cost from 2 to 30. Ablations bound the claim: the gain comes from the Soft-block band, and a Bayes cost rule is cheaper still (2.164). The contribution is an auditable decision layer with a decision-centric evaluation, not a new detector.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Security operations centres (SOCs) centralise the handling of security alerts~\cite{vielberth2020}, yet analysts receive more alerts than they can investigate, and alert fatigue is a central operational risk~\cite{tariq2025,chhetri2024}. Intrusion-detection research, from classical data mining~\cite{liao2013,buczak2016} to deep networks on NSL-KDD~\cite{vinayakumar2019,su2020}, usually stops at a score judged by accuracy or the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC). A SOC manager must instead decide, under finite analyst capacity and asymmetric error costs, which alerts to monitor, contain or escalate: a data-driven decision task~\cite{vial2019,duan2019}.

Fuzzy and neuro-fuzzy systems suit this step because they express learned evidence as linguistic rules that operators can audit~\cite{zadeh1965,jang1993,souza2020,fernandez2019}. Table~\ref{tab:rw} shows the gap. Fuzzy intrusion detectors use rules to raise accuracy but output a binary alarm~\cite{toosi2007,hamamoto2018,alalhareth2023}; cost-sensitive learning~\cite{elkan2001,zhou2006} enters intrusion detection through training losses~\cite{gupta2022,telikani2024}, not through the choice of action; explainable AI (XAI) explains scores after the fact~\cite{arrieta2020,wang2020xai,alabbadi2025}; and reinforcement-learning prioritisation ranks alerts without assigning actions~\cite{wang2024alertpro}. Knowledge-based decision support~\cite{bag2025} shows the value of explicit knowledge, but none of these studies combines an action-cost model, readable rules and graded responses.

\section{Proposed fuzzy--neural decision framework}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Neural propensity model}
Each alert $i$ has features $x_i$ (38 numeric NSL-KDD fields, standardised; protocol, service and flag, one-hot encoded) and label $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ (normal, attack). An MLP $g_\theta$ with ReLU layers of width 128 and 64, dropout 0.2 and one logit output gives the attack propensity
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
p_i&=\sigma\big(g_\theta(x_i)\big),\\
\mathcal{L}(\theta)&=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[w_{+}\,y_i\log p_i+(1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big],\qquad w_{+}=\frac{N_{-}}{N_{+}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:mlp}
\end{equation}
where $N_{-}$ and $N_{+}$ count normal and attack training records. Training uses Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 512, 20 epochs) and keeps the epoch with the best validation ROC-AUC.

\subsection{Fuzzy risk bands and production-rule base}
Propensity is mapped to three linguistic risk bands~\cite{zadeh1965} by shoulder and triangular membership functions, with $[z]_0^1=\min(1,\max(0,z))$:
\begin{align}
\mu_L(p)&=\big[(0.45-p)/0.45\big]_0^1, \label{eq:muL}\\
\mu_M(p)&=\begin{cases}\big[(p-0.15)/0.35\big]_0^1, & p<0.5,\\ \big[(0.85-p)/0.35\big]_0^1, & p\ge 0.5,\end{cases} \label{eq:muM}\\
\mu_H(p)&=\big[(p-0.55)/0.45\big]_0^1. \label{eq:muH}
\end{align}
The knowledge layer is the production-rule base $\mathcal{R}=\{R_1,R_2,R_3\}$:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.88\linewidth}@{}}
$R_1$: & IF risk IS Low THEN action $=$ Monitor (log the alert; no intervention);\\
$R_2$: & IF risk IS Medium THEN action $=$ Soft-block (automatic containment, e.g., rate limit);\\
$R_3$: & IF risk IS High THEN action $=$ Escalate (hand over to a human analyst).
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Rule $R_k$ fires with strength $\mu_k(p_i)$; conflicts are resolved by winner-take-all, with ties going to the less severe action:
\begin{equation}
a_i = A\big(k_i^{*}\big),\qquad k_i^{*}=\arg\max_{k\in(L,M,H)}\mu_k(p_i),
\label{eq:rule}
\end{equation}
where $A$ maps $(L,M,H)$ to (Monitor, Soft-block, Escalate). Firing strengths are logged with every decision as its explanation (e.g., ``$p=0.60$: $\mu_M=0.71$, $\mu_H=0.11$, Soft-block''). Solving $\mu_L=\mu_M$ and $\mu_M=\mu_H$ shows that Eq.~(\ref{eq:rule}) switches action at $p=9/32=0.281$ and $p=23/32=0.719$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:bands}). The breakpoints were set by expert judgement before the test data were examined.

\subsection{Cost model and decision metrics}
Actions are scored with the relative cost matrix
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
C(\text{Monitor},y)&=C_{FN}\,y, & C(\text{Soft-block},y)&=1+(1-y),\\
C(\text{Escalate},y)&=3+3(1-y), & C_{FN}&=10,
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
so a missed attack costs 10 units, containing an attack 1 unit and escalating a benign alert 6 units. Policies are compared by the mean triage cost $\bar C=\frac1N\sum_i C(a_i,y_i)$, the attack coverage $\sum_i \mathbf{1}[a_i\neq\text{Monitor}]\,y_i/\sum_i y_i$ and the number of unnecessary escalations (UE), i.e., escalated benign alerts. As a reference, we also report the plug-in Bayes cost rule
\begin{equation}
a_i^{B}=\arg\min_{a}\;\big[(1-p_i)\,C(a,0)+p_i\,C(a,1)\big],
\label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
which treats $p_i$ as a calibrated probability, an assumption that deep-model scores do not guarantee~\cite{abdar2021}. Because $C(\text{Soft-block},y)<C(\text{Escalate},y)$ for both classes, Eq.~(\ref{eq:bayes}) never escalates and soft-blocks whenever $p_i>2/11$.

\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}
\textit{Data.} We use the NSL-KDD benchmark~\cite{tavallaee2009}. For CPU-scale reproducibility, 40,000 training records are sampled and split (stratified) into 34,000 training and 6,000 validation records; the scaler and encoder are fitted on training data only. The test set is capped at 20,000 records (attack rate 0.568) and contains attack types absent from training, so train/test shift is part of the task.

\textit{Baselines.} The detectors are class-balanced logistic regression, gradient boosting (GB)~\cite{friedman2001} with scikit-learn defaults and the MLP alone; the proposed policy shares the MLP propensity, so any benefit must appear in decisions. Decision baselines act on the same scores: a Monitor/Escalate threshold at 0.5, the same threshold tuned on validation data to minimise cost (threshold moving~\cite{zhou2006}) and escalation of the top 20\% of scores, a fixed analyst-capacity rule. The Bayes cost rule is a reference only; learned policies~\cite{wang2024alertpro} need interaction data and are excluded.

\textit{Statistics.} On the seed-42 test set, we report percentile 95\% confidence intervals (CIs) from 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of alerts~\cite{efron1994} and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on per-alert cost differences~\cite{wilcoxon1945}. The full pipeline is repeated for seeds 42--51 (paired Wilcoxon test across seeds), and two sweeps vary the missed-attack cost $C_{FN}$ from 2 to 30 and the Medium-band half-width from 0.25 to 0.45.

\textit{Prediction versus decision.} Gradient boosting is the best detector on every metric (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Panel A) and beats the MLP in ROC-AUC in all ten seeds; we therefore make no detection claim. On identical MLP scores, the rule base lowers the mean triage cost by 0.196 per alert relative to the 0.5 threshold and by 1.242 relative to top-20\% escalation, and raises coverage by 0.031 (95\% CI [0.028, 0.034]) over the threshold (Panel B1). Rule base and threshold differ only on the 776 Medium-band alerts, which the rule base contains; this saves about 3,900 cost units per 20,000 alerts and sends 387 fewer alerts to analysts. Top-20\% escalation minimises unnecessary escalations (47) but covers only 0.348 of attacks, and validation tuning ($t=0.53$; 3.278) does not close the gap because unseen attack types shift the test scores.

\textit{Robustness.} The rule base is cheaper than both crisp rules in all ten seeds ($3.088\pm0.081$ versus $3.246\pm0.076$ and $4.318\pm0.009$; Wilcoxon $p=0.002$ for both) and has higher coverage than the 0.5 threshold in every seed. Its advantage over the threshold grows from 0.055 at $C_{FN}=2$ to 0.549 at $C_{FN}=30$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}); top-20\% escalation is cheaper only for $C_{FN}\le3$ (1.347 versus 1.640 at $C_{FN}=2$). Widening the Medium half-width from 0.25 to 0.45 enlarges the Soft-block band from 646 to 900 alerts and lowers the cost monotonically from 3.101 to 3.034; every width beats both crisp rules.

\textit{What drives the gain.} Crisp bands at $p=9/32$ and $23/32$ reproduce every action (Panel B2), so the benefit comes from the containment band, not from fuzziness itself; the memberships add a graded explanation per decision. The rule base is not cost-optimal: the Bayes cost rule is 0.903 cheaper per alert because soft-blocking dominates escalation under Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}), so it contains 8,631 alerts (43\% of traffic). An oracle test-set threshold of at most 0.10 would also win (minimum 2.929 at $t=0.01$), but validation tuning cannot find it under shift. On GB scores, the same rules cost 3.004 ($p=0.017$ versus MLP scores) and cut unnecessary escalations from 603 to 222, so the layer transfers.

\textit{Implications for SOC decision-makers.} First, detectors should be judged by the decisions they enable: on identical scores, the action rule changed the cost by up to 1.242 per alert. Second, the cost matrix sets the action mix, so organisations must price false containment before automating Soft-block. Third, band boundaries are a reviewable workload lever that needs no retraining. Fourth, named rules with logged firing strengths give each action an audit trail that post hoc attributions~\cite{wang2020xai,alabbadi2025} lack, aiding compliance and human--AI teaming~\cite{chhetri2024}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
Fixed bands are sensitive to the score distribution, not only to the ranking: seed 48, the weakest replicate (cost 3.282, coverage 0.626), has a typical ROC-AUC (0.900) but the lowest accuracy at 0.5 (0.757). Adaptive, ANFIS-style tuning~\cite{jang1993} or cost-derived bands could close the Bayes gap. NSL-KDD (2009) is a dated laboratory benchmark that misses modern enterprise, cloud and Internet of Things traffic~\cite{ring2019,algaradi2020}, and the data were subsampled under one protocol. The cost matrix is illustrative, not elicited from a SOC; coverage counts containment as covering an attack; and there was no deployment, drift analysis or user study.

%% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:concl}
As a knowledge-based decision layer, the fuzzy--neural rule base outperformed crisp threshold and capacity rules on NSL-KDD in cost and coverage across seeds, cost ratios and band widths. The gain comes from the containment band, and a Bayes rule derived from the cost matrix is cheaper still. Future work will derive bands from elicited SOC costs and validate them live.
